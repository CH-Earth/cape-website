The Water Institute and the UNU Hub are in the midst of applying their water prediction capabilities in different countries to demonstrate how advances in hydrological modelling and forecasting can be translated into practical decision support across diverse operational and institutional settings. A central objective is to reduce the technical, computational, and data barriers that have historically limited widespread application of state-of-the-art modeling capabilities. Applying these models across diverse hydrological and climatic settings creates a powerful learning environment, exposing model limitations, testing assumptions, and generating insights that improve water prediction systems.

Six researchers were asked the following questions about their experiences with international research collaborations:
1.	Who have you worked with internationally as part of your research?
2.	What is the nature of your work with international partners?
3.	What is the impact of your international work?
4.	Why is it important to build international partnerships for your research or work?

Cyril Thébault:
During my two years at the University of Calgary, I worked mainly with collaborators involved in CIROH, including Yalan Song and Chaopeng Shen from Penn State (US), Shaun Carney and Katie van Werkhoven from RTI International (US), Mukesh Kumar from the University of Alabama (US), and Andy Wood and Andy Newman from NCAR (US). I also collaborated with Nans Addor from Fathom (UK). Through other publications where I was involved as a co-author, I have also worked closely with Simon Mathias from Durham University (UK) and Luca Lombardo from Politecnico di Torino (Italy). More recently, since moving to Earth Sciences New Zealand (NZ), I have been mostly working with colleagues here, particularly Céline Cattoën, while continuing to work within Martyn’s research team. Most of these collaborations have been centred around joint research projects and scientific publications. They involve sharing expertise, discussing methodological approaches, analysing and interpreting results, sharing data and simulations, and developing papers together. These collaborations have helped me learn new approaches, broaden my research perspective, and build a wider professional network. They have also contributed to joint publications and helped connect my work with a broader international research community. It is important to develop research that is relevant beyond a single location. International partnerships make it possible to bring together different datasets, methods, and regional experiences, which can help make research more robust and transferable to different contexts.

Peter Wagener:
Most of the existing collaborations stem from research visits I did this year. These are with Prof. Seibert's group at the University of Zurich, Switzerland and Prof. Wagener's group in Potsdam, Germany. Specifically, I am working primarily with Franziska Clerc-Schwarzenbach, a PhD student at the University of Zurich, and Prof. Wagener at the University of Potsdam. I previously worked with Prof. Schütze's group at TU Dresden, Germany, where I completed my master’s degree. In most cases, these collaborations are centred on research in hydrologic analysis and/or modelling. More specifically, the research is focused on comparing large-sample datasets across different locations (e.g., North America and France) and using hydrologic signatures to constrain hydrologic behaviour. The outcomes of this work aim to improve decision-making in hydrological modelling by highlighting benefits and drawbacks of datasets and modelling choices. For example, the research answers questions such as how model parameters compensate for biases in the forcing data and how the choice of objective function affects which signatures a model reproduces well. This should make our predictions more reliable across large domains and under changing conditions. The most important benefit of international partnerships is getting additional perspectives on the challenges you are working on. There is a reason specific to hydrology; large-sample datasets are assembled nationally or continentally, so testing whether a finding holds beyond a single country requires partners who know the data and landscapes locally. Furthermore, people with different backgrounds or education can provide valuable insights that help advance and improve the work on both sides.

Anagha Peringiyil:
During my PhD, I collaborated with Andy Wood at the National Center for Atmospheric Research (NCAR), USA, to develop an ensemble precipitation dataset over India using GMET/GPEP models. My collaborations with international partners have focused on joint research initiatives, knowledge sharing, and methodological advancement. I worked with Andy Wood to implement the Gridded Meteorological Ensemble Tool (GMET) for India, with the objective of developing an ensemble precipitation dataset. The collaboration involved detailed discussions on the GMET methodology, model implementation, interpretation of ensemble outputs, and assessment of uncertainties associated with meteorological forcing and its influence on hydrological simulations. This experience enhanced my research skills in hydrometeorological data and ensemble-based precipitation analysis. It also deepened my understanding of uncertainty sources in hydrological modeling, particularly those related to meteorological forcing and their propagation through hydrological simulations. The broader impact of international research collaboration significantly advances the development and exchange of datasets, methodologies, and scientific knowledge, thereby benefiting the global hydrological research community. For example, the development of a national-scale ensemble precipitation dataset for India using GMET/GPEP provides a framework for representing uncertainty in precipitation estimates and supporting more robust hydrological modeling and hydroclimatic studies. Developing methodologies and analytical approaches to better understand, quantify, and reduce uncertainties in hydrological modeling has broader relevance, as these approaches can be adapted and applied across different regions and hydroclimatic conditions. More broadly, international collaboration promotes the exchange of expertise and approaches across countries and helps address global challenges related to climate variability, extreme events, and water-resource management. International collaborations are essential because they promote the exchange of ideas, methodological innovation, and access to a broad range of expertise. Hydrological and climate-related challenges often extend beyond geographical boundaries; therefore, working with researchers from various countries introduces new perspectives and approaches to addressing complex issues. These collaborations also foster interdisciplinary research, broaden professional networks, and help establish strong relationships that may lead to future joint projects and publications. In my experience, addressing complex hydrological problems requires a team with diverse expertise and viewpoints, rather than relying on a single individual, which is time-consuming, labour-intensive, and often lacks constructive feedback.

Nico Vásquez:
While in Chile doing my PhD, I visited Dr. Martyn Clark when he was at the University of Saskatchewan. Now that I am in Canada, we have ongoing collaborations with University de Chile, specifically with Dr. Pablo Mendoza. Overall, I implement hydrological modeling and calibration strategies and develop datasets aiming to better estimate streamflow in ungauged areas and under-represented regions (e.g., South America). I hope our impact will be two-fold: 1) verify that frameworks developed here in Calgary can be applied to a broader range of hydroclimates and hydrological regimes, and 2) expand the data availability to speed research in underrepresented regions of the world, particularly South America. The latter also aims to make science more robust by reducing bias. It is important to build international partnerships because frameworks and new theories prove robust when they can be applied across a wide range of conditions. That requires data, and data is unevenly distributed across the globe. Additionally, planet Earth has interconnections that usually require analyzing large domains. When such domains lack sufficient data, we need to transfer information and knowledge from data-rich to data-poor regions. However, hydrological estimates can only be tested and qualify as good when local knowledge is considered in the analysis. Finally, different regions of the world face similar water problems (e.g., droughts, pollution, etc.) and have addressed them using different approaches. Thus, maintaining a research ecosystem where researchers from different parts of the world share what does and does not work helps find better solutions to shared problems.

Ignacio Aguirre:
During my research, I have had the opportunity to collaborate with scientists at the National Center for Atmospheric Research (NCAR) in Boulder, USA, and the UK Centre for Ecology & Hydrology (UKCEH) in Wallingford, UK. These collaborations have focused on advancing calibration methods for process-based terrestrial models, including leveraging machine-learning approaches to improve model performance while preserving their physical basis, as well as better characterizing model uncertainty. This research has broad relevance because process-based models are widely used around the world to support applications such as flood and drought assessment, weather and climate prediction, water resource management, and climate change impact assessment. During my visit to NCAR, my work focused on testing calibration methods for the Community Land Model under different representations of physical processes, while at UKCEH I worked with the JULES land-surface model, with a particular focus on regionalization methods. Broad international scientific communities use both models; therefore, advancing methods that produce more realistic and accurate simulations has applications well beyond the institutions where the research was conducted. International partnerships have also been particularly valuable for identifying new research questions and model limitations. For example, during my visit to UKCEH, we identified mismatches in the simulation of sensible heat across regions worldwide. Investigating these differences through discussions with researchers from diverse backgrounds led us to develop a new approach for representing stomatal resistance, aimed at improving the representation of land–atmosphere interactions. Opportunities such as this illustrate why international partnerships are essential to my research: environmental challenges are global, but their characteristics, impacts, and priorities vary across regions. Working internationally has provided me with exposure to diverse perspectives and connected me with researchers with expertise across disciplines, including hydrology, meteorology, ecology, biology, computational science, and the social sciences. Bringing together this diversity of perspectives and expertise can reveal new research questions, lead to more broadly applicable solutions, and enable scientific advances that would be difficult to achieve within a single discipline, institution, or country.

Neharika Bhattarai:
As a visiting student researcher from IIT Delhi, I work with Martyn Clark and his team on large-sample hydrological model calibration and regionalization across the Indian Subcontinent, including basins that lack observed streamflow data. The collaboration gives me access to UCalgary's computing infrastructure, which lets me run model calibrations across hundreds of basins at once, along with the team's modelling expertise and experience from large-sample hydrological studies conducted globally, which I bring back to my work across the Indian Subcontinent. This has produced calibration and regionalization methods that hold up across a large, diverse set of basins, including ones with no observed streamflow data. Building this kind of collaboration matters because it introduces me to methods, tools, and perspectives I wouldn't otherwise have access to at IIT Delhi, and that helps the research move further and faster than it would on its own.
